I. Black water in ancient mythology

We believe that since the place names in the “Mountain and Sea Sutra” do not correspond to ancient landforms, then the problem of black water's hope is not necessarily a geographical issue; since black water is always linked to mysterious countries and mysterious things, and to an imagination about life, then we might as well imagine that its aspiration problem is a mythological issue. The essence of black water is expressed through “immortality.” So where did this essence come from? Although existing sources do not provide a direct answer to this, the question can be solved by linking to ancient turtle myths. The book “Chu Zong Temple Murals and Turtles Trace Map” once talked about turtle worship in ancient China. I think it was one of the earliest forms of animal worship developed in China. Turtle worship was once popular as a form of totem in the Yangtze River Basin and parts of the Yellow River Basin in China, and crystallized into the turtle spirit concept. Since it has ancient roots, only Xuanwu has a clear prototype in the worship of the Four Spirits. Due to the longevity of turtles and the ability to grow in soil and water, turtles were used as a medium for the ancients to understand gods, used in various witchcraft rituals, and used as a tool for divination. Beginning in the late Yin Dynasty, the turtle was combined with the concept of ancestral spirits and the idea of underworld, giving the turtle the status of a symbol of the gods of God of God, the god of the North, and ancestral spirits. Corresponsively, the ancient totem of the Yin people, the owl (brown bird), was also regarded by the ancients as a “three-legged crow” and a western bird due to its nocturnal characteristics and “squatting” characteristics. The status of a turtle and a turtle made the ancients imagine the sun at night, and the turtle as a god carrying the sun in the dark, believing that they shared the mission of sending the sun back to the East. Based on the above understanding, we can judge that the so-called “black water” is actually the path that the Sun or the Sun in the underworld traveled at night according to the ancients. IV. Conclusions
Place names such as Heishui, Kunlun, and Penglai are originally several symbols in the ancient Chinese underworld mythology system, each representing a different state of life. Their ideas are based on ancient turtle worship, sun worship, and underworld beliefs. Black water originally refers to the passage of the sun back from the west to the east at night, thus representing the flow and eternity of life. Kunlun originally refers to a black tomb, so it represents death: conceived as the home of the Sun and all life, placed in the northwest corner, called “virtual,” and viewed as the home of the Yudu and emperors. Horai originally refers to the mountain of the rising sun. It resembles the image of a turtle owl (sunbird), thus representing the regeneration of life. The relationship between these three is that Horai is a sacred mountain corresponding to Kunlun, and black water is a link connecting Mount Nikami. When the ancients imagined the turtle as an angel carrying the sun back to the East through dark water, two sacred mountains modeled after turtles appeared at both ends of the earth in the ancients, that is, the east and west poles of black water — Kunlun in the northwest had the character of darkness and death, and became a mountain of punishment; Horai in the southeast had the character of daytime and regeneration, and became a mountain of paradise. Therefore, in Horai mythology, we can see traces of Kunlun and black water; in the various myths mentioned above, we can all see traces of turtles.
For the time being limited, we have omitted the discussion on ancient turtle worship. However, the above discussion on the original meaning of Heishui, Kunlun, and Penglai can prove that the myth of a turtle one-day system or life worship system existed during the pre-Qin era. This is one of the most important mythological systems in ancient China. This mythological system shows the efforts of the ancients in understanding the order of the universe. They “take everything from afar, take everything from near”, regard the operation of the Sun and the cycle of human life as the core of the cosmic order, thus establishing knowledge about time and space, which gave rise to primitive geography, astronomy, calendar science, and other disciplines. Although all of this knowledge is shrouded in mystical religion, there is no doubt that they have initially moved away from the limitations of primitive beliefs with totem worship, nature worship, and ancestor worship as the main contents, and have taken a step towards establishing a more abstract symbolic system.
In the above sense, “black water,” “Kunlun,” and “Horai” can also be seen as a few terms relating to ancient cosmology. Like terms such as “Qinglong,” “White Tiger,” “Suzaku,” “Xuanwu,” “Qian,” “Kun,” “Zhen,” “Xun,” “Kan,” “Li,” “Geng,” and “Tui,” they laid the foundation for the birth of classical Chinese philosophy. They also show that between primitive beliefs and the philosophies of many, ancient Chinese people experienced a stage where specific names were used to express abstract concepts; the process of Chinese thought can therefore be described as (1) figurative object symbols, (2) figurative object symbols, and (3) abstract object symbols gradually evolving. Therefore, traces of the second stage can still be seen in terms such as “Tao” and “Qi” in Chinese philosophy. Part of the purpose of writing this article is to point out the existence of this “second stage.” In other words, this article hopes to establish this understanding through the interpretation of several place names in ancient mythology: when we examine ancient culture, we must not only avoid understanding things in early civilizations according to the way historians usually think and express modern people; but also avoid simply explaining these things as “primitive thinking” or “primitive beliefs,” as mythologists usually do. Early Chinese civilizations and the special ways of thinking and expression they used required a specific discipline to study.
