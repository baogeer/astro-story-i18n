 “Eighteen Shots of Hu” is a heartbreaking song from time immemorial. Its author is Choi Man-hee. To enjoy this poem, don't read it as ordinary written literature; think that Tsai Wenji, an unlucky woman, is singing on her own, and the sound of the piano is flowing along with her heart. With the sound of the piano and singing, we seem to be walking a long path paved with humiliation and pain...
She began to show up in front of the turmoil of the times. The first shot was a bit “chaotic” in the background: Hulu is strong, the flames are everywhere, and the soldiers are in exile. The world was in turmoil in Han and Wei. Eunuchs, foreigners, and warlords took control of the DPRK government one after another. Peasants' uprisings, warlord scuffles, and foreign invasions continued one after another. In Han Wei's poem, “Armor lice are born, and thousands of people die. White bones are in the wild, and no chicken sounds for a thousand miles.” Wait, these are all true portrayals of the unrest at the time. Tsai Wen-ji was kidnapped west by Hu in the midst of military chaos.
Being beaten was the beginning of her painful career, and it was also the root of her painful career. Therefore, in the poem, I dedicated the second photo to write about her situation during her abduction, and also used “a lifetime of hard work and separation,” to indicate that the hardships of a lifetime stemmed from being beaten. She was forced to stay in the Southern Huns for 12 years and endured great suffering in life and mentality. Hu Di's nature is harsh: “Hu Feng Hao Hao,” “Frost is raging,” “wilderness depression,” and “crying in running water,” a strange lifestyle is out of place for her. Wearing clothes made of fur, my heart was shocked: “I was shocked to the bone by the felt fur clothes.” I eat meat and milk. The fishy smell is so bad that I can't swallow it. “The taste is futile to contain my feelings.” They have no place to live. They migrate after aquatic plants, and live in temporary sheds made of grass rafts and dried cow and sheep dung; when excited, they drummed and sang and danced, making loud noises and going all night long. In short, she was unable to adapt to Hu Di's harsh natural environment, nor was she able to endure the living habits of the Hu people, which is very different from the Han people. Therefore, she sang the painful voice of “Different customs, different physical difficulties, different desires, who can speak to you”, and what made her most unbearable was the spiritual aspect.
Spiritually, she experienced double humiliation: as a Han, she became a prisoner of the Hu people; as a woman, she was forced to marry the Hu people. The connotation of the first film, called “Good Will, Justice, Loss,” refers precisely to this double humiliation. In a situation where both body and mind were being tormented, missing her hometown and thinking back became the most important spiritual strength to support her to live a strong life. The main content from the second shoot to the eleventh shoot was to write about her homesickness. The fourth shot, “Never Miss My Homeland,” the tenth shot, “No Sound Is Dead by My Hometown, I'll Swallow in Silence”, and the eleventh shot, “Life Still Wants to Return to Sang” are all moving words that directly tell a sense of homesickness. However, the most moving expression of homesickness is the fifth shot. In this shoot, Choi Man-ki unearthed a far-fetched and profound situation with her obsessive affection: in the fall, she looked forward to the blue, expecting the giant geese flying from the south to carry her feelings; in spring, she looked up at the clouds and looked forward to the news of her native land brought by giant geese returning north. However, the giant geese flew away from the factory, and it was hard to find them. She couldn't help but feel heartbroken and lost her soul... In the eleventh shoot, she revealed the secrets of her heart where she endured humiliation and stealing life: “I'm not greedy to die; I can't donate my body and heart for good. I still hope to return to Sang Zi in my life; I have died for too long.” Finally, after a long period of 12 years, her wish to return to her hometown came true. “When an envoy of Han called Jinxi, he called Jin Xian and sent a thousand Jinxi to redeem her concubine.” But this joy was fleeting. While she was overjoyed, a new cloud of sorrow floated over her. She thought about the day she survived, which was also a time to say goodbye to her two biological sons. As stated in the twelfth shot: “I'm happy to be alive and met Sheng Jun; there is no reason to say goodbye to being a child. It is a candid explanation of this paradoxical mentality. “It is difficult to express the love between two people.” Beginning on the 13th shoot, Choi Man-ki moved on to depicting not being able to bear to be separated from her son. “Eighteen Shots of Hu Wei” not only reflects Tsai Wen-ji's lack of life, but also her talent. “Eighteen Shots of Hu” has strong subjective and lyrical colors in the creation of the main character, Tsai Wenji's own artistic image, even in terms of narration. It was written about being exiled to the West, having two children in Hu Di, returning home from home, and returning to Chang'an, all with affectionate admiration.
