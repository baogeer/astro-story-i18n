Relying on both soft and hard methods, Emperor Han Wu unified his government, unified his finances, unified his armed forces, and unified his ideals. As a result, he developed the centralized imperial system to its first peak. Under him, the empire's cultural and martial arts reached its peak. The territory was vast and culturally prosperous. The Silk Road stretched thousands of miles directly to Europe, Chinese civilization spread all over the world, and the Han Empire became the most powerful country in the world at the time.
However, during the time of Emperor Han Wu, the crisis of the empire had already unwittingly come to light. As mentioned earlier, an empire is a mature form and typical form of a power society, and its characteristic is centralization of power. Although centralized power is not necessarily an authoritarian system, and authoritarianism is not necessarily a dictatorship, Emperor Han Wu is mostly an authoritarian and dictator. Moreover, precisely because of his authoritarianism and dictatorship, not only did North Korea's Chinese ministers and local county governors often decide to relieve themselves, but even Prince Liu Ji and Empress Wei Zifu finally committed suicide. As a result, when this government collapsed, only an eight-year-old child (i.e. Emperor Liu Fuling of Zhao) succeeded to the throne. The empire's supreme power, including the power to abolish the emperor, has since fallen into the hands of foreign relatives — first by General Huo Guang, then by Grand Sima Wang Mang. They either regard abolition as a child's play, or see the emperor as a doll.
Although the Han dynasty came back to life through Emperor Guangwu Liu Xiu's “Zhongxing,” it was difficult for the entire empire to improve, but it was only barely maintained. The 200-year history of the post-Han dynasty is almost scarce. The first three emperors, namely Guangwu Emperor Liu Xiu, Ming Emperor Liu Zhuang, and Emperor Zhang Liu Yi, made a difference. The other emperors were either weak-crowned or taken to charge in their infancy. Four of them didn't even have time to celebrate their 10th birthday, and the supreme power of the empire was either controlled by relatives, or stolen by lieutenants, or disrupted by the heroine. By the time the ambitious warlord Dong Zhuo led the so-called “Qin Wang's Division” into Beijing to kill the lieutenants, he simply killed the Han Empire as well. According to “The Book of the Later Han Dynasty? “The Age of Emperor Xian”. In July of the first year of Jian'an (196 AD), when the abducted Emperor Han Xian returned to Luoyang, it was actually “a hundred officials overcome obstacles” and “starved to death between walls”. The splendid weather during Emperor Wu is now safe? But I saw, “Remains of the westerly wind, Han Family Mausoleum.”
Maybe this is called “boom and fall”?
This process was repeated again in the Tang Empire, only faster and more dramatic. It is a history that is constantly mentioned, written and interpreted over and over again, and reviewed in various forms (works, essays, novels, poetry, drama, movies), so it is particularly worth reflecting on.
Tang is the second highest peak in the history of the empire. Its splendor is known as the “Han and Tang Meteorology” along with Han and Tang. However, while Han's great achievements were mostly native and native, Tang's achievements were multi-ethnic and international. In other words, compared to Han, who pioneered the “Silk Road,” Tang's achievements are more multi-ethnic and international. Please see the list.
Western countries conquered by the Sui and Tang dynasties were: Gaochang (Turpan, Xinjiang), Kizi (Kuqa, Xinjiang), Dangxiang (western Gansu), and Tuguhun (Qinghai). Trade and friendship are maintained with Yanqi (Yanqi, Xinjiang), Shule (Kashgar, Xinjiang), Yu Qin (Hetian, Xinjiang), Tianzhu (India), Qin (Kashmir), Kangguo (central Asia), Persia (Iran), Daishu (Arabian Peninsula), Gantang (south of the Caspian Sea), Zhu Juba (north of the Green Ridge), Siburu (Nepal), Shi Guo (northern Central Asia), and Daqin (Eastern Roman Empire). These are still just Western countries. Others include Goryeo (northeastern part of the Korean peninsula), Baekje (southwestern part of the Korean peninsula), Bohai Sea (within the two provinces of Liaoning and Jilin), Khitan (northeastern Hebei and parts of Liaoning), Tufan (Tibet), Turks (northern China), Nanyuan (Yunnan), etc. They are either conquered, or come to pay tribute, or trade. The Tang Empire opened its doors, welcomed them without discrimination, and learned from each other without discrimination.
This is a cosmopolitan superpower. As a result, Chang'an, Kyoto, of the Tang Empire became an authentic cosmopolitan metropolis, filled with bizarre “star-chasers” from all over the world in bizarre costumes. The politics, military, and culture of the Tang Empire were all objects they fervently adored and competed to imitate. The Japanese are the most passionate of them. The “ambassadors to Tang” they sent first had 3,500 people each time, then 2,3,000 each time. In addition to the chief and deputy envoys, there are also a large number of international students and academic monks. Some don't go back when they come; others copy them as soon as they go back. Kyoto and Nara were modeled after Chang'an, but they are far short of scale and scope.
Isn't Datang the heyday?
There are many reasons why the Tang became the golden age of the empire. Wei Jin's unrest in the North and South Dynasties for more than 300 years is one reason (we'll talk about this later), and the unification of China by the Sui Dynasty is also one reason. Without the vitality of Wei Jin and the North and South Dynasties injected in the year 369, and without Sui's entrepreneurship laying the foundation, there would not have been the heyday of the Tang Dynasty. In this sense, Tang Taizong's so-called “Zhen Guan Zhi Zhi” was nothing more than picking a peach. Of course, being able to pick peaches, rather than throwing them away like Emperor Sui Yi, should be said to have been due to Li Shimin's ingenuity and reform and opening up.
Li Shimin's very powerful move was to uphold, develop, and improve the two systems founded by the Sui dynasty — the academic examination system and the government system. The “Election Chronicles” of the “New Tang Shu” begins by saying, “Most of the leaders of the Tang dynasty came from Sui Jiu.” One of the important rules is that “all scholars are listed in states and counties”, which means they are free to register for public examinations based on their household registration or birth certificate, and become candidates for national civil service based on the results of the examination. This is the academic examination system.
The academic examination system is a talent selection system. In ancient China, it was called an “election system” (selection means choice, promotion). This kind of system is far more reasonable and more beneficial to the country than the investigation (investigation and expropriation) of the Qin and Han dynasties and the recommendations of Wei Jin and the North and South Dynasties (Jiupin Zhongzheng). We'll talk more about its significance later (detailed in chapter 3 of the book). What I want to say here is that precisely because the academic examination system is beneficial not only to talent selection, but also to centralization of power, it was also inherited by later dynasties and regarded as a top priority relating to the fate of the dynasty, and continued until 1905 AD.
Simultaneously with this, since the beginning of the Sui Dynasty, county officials (deputy positions) were no longer self-appointed (chosen by themselves) by the chief (official position). “Officials, big and small, are handled by the Ministry of Public Affairs,” that is, all officials are appointed by the central government. This is unquestionably aimed at strengthening centralization. Another important system, also founded by Sui, is the “three provinces and six parts system,” that is, the central government is divided into three provinces: Shang Shu, China, and Sheng Shu. Among them, the Shang Shu province is also divided into six departments of government, household, courtesy, military, punishment, and labor. China Shu Province is responsible for processing downstream documents, and the lower province is responsible for reviewing upstream affairs. Shangshu Province has a total of six departments and is responsible for all administrative work. Seen in this way, the Chinese Shu Province is a bit like the legislature, the province under China is a bit like the supervisory department, and the Shang Shu Province is a bit like the administrative department.
However, the significance of the “three provinces and six part system” cannot be underestimated. We know that any unified dynasty in Chinese history was a giant empire. Any emperor, whether wise or bad, must and can only rely on a bureaucratic group to manage the country. The number of people in this bureaucratic group has varied from generation to generation, but even if the military were simplified, it would still be too large for that “lonely person.” Without a minimum check and balance of power, and without corresponding institutional arrangements, the operation of power cannot be controlled.
Therefore, at the beginning of the establishment of the imperial system, there was a “separation of powers” of administration, military affairs, and supervision; up to the Sui and Tang dynasties, there was also a “division of power among the three provinces” under Shang Shu, China, and his family. This is because although the three powers of administration, military affairs, and supervision are divided, the executive power (relative power) still seems too heavy, and there is a risk that it will deter the monarchy. This requires another division, that is, reducing the relative weight. After the founding of the Song Dynasty, due to the political disorder in the late Tang dynasty, Taizu had excessive power and domination at the end of the Tang dynasty. Outside, he took over all the power of the region to the central government, regained the power of the central government to the monarch, implemented a “two-government system” between the Chinese province and the Privy Council, which further used the Privy Council to control the Chinese province (the Chinese Shu Province in the Song Dynasty actually included Tang Zhishang Shu, China, and the three provinces under China).
In the Ming Dynasty, Zhu Yuanzhang simply abolished the prime minister. The emperor became the head of state and also the head of government, and even more centralized power. Of course, this is an afterword (more on that below). In short, the academic examination system selects officials, and the three provincial systems limit power. With such a foundation, the Tang Empire can implement its policy of full opening-up to the outside world.

Syrians, Arabs, Persians, Tatars, Tibetans, Koreans, Japanese, and Annan people all coexist peacefully and work together here (see Hwang In-woo's “Talking about Chinese History on the River Husson”).
