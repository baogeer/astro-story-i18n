This is an amazing story, but it's not entirely fictional; it's based on historical facts. There is indeed a “very interesting” Ji Kang in history (in Qu Yang language).
When we talk about Ji Kang, we also think of Wei Jin's merry. In a sense, Ji Kang is even Wei Jin's ambassador. “The World Speaks a New Language” discusses Ji Kang's 21 articles. The four most important subjects of morality, speech, politics, and literature all have related deeds, depicting Ji Kang's elegance in every aspect. Even his son Ji Shao is famous as “Qingyuan Yazheng.” However, Wei Jin was not only an elegant person; Ji Kang was also not just an aesthetic object. Wei Jin was a cruel and bloody era, and Ji Kang was a victim of this era.
Professor Wang Xiaoyi of the Department of History at Tsinghua University's “The Biography of Ji Kang” describes the conflicted, rich, and three-dimensional life of this victim in a special era. Ji Kang's books already on the market are either too many novelists or too much fiction, or are either too long and slightly cumbersome, either scattered and unintegrated, or focus on thought and light life. Wang Xiaoyi's “The Biography of Ji Kang” is only 160,000 words long, but it can integrate rigor and poetry, blend life and thought in one place, and systematically restore Ji Kang's life course and thought path, and even Wei Jin's sense of the times, Ji Kang's life and thoughts, and even Kang Ji's friends and enemies Very vividly portrayed.
As an expert on Wei Jin's history, Wang Xiaoyi used a combination of ideological history and political history in “The Biography of Ji Kang”. The investigation was rigorous, the description was clear, and he wrote about the ups and downs of individual lives in the great era. This book not only focuses on outlining the story of the Wei Jin era and Ji Kang's life. In just a few strokes, it shows the temperament of the times and Ji Kang's temperament, and also presents all aspects of Ji Kang's thought in close text. Through argumentative articles such as “No Sound, No Sadness,” “Health Theory,” and “Bad House, No Good, No Evil,” you will find Ji Kang's strong critical thinking. He is not only a poet, but also a philosopher. On the one hand, the exuberant debates during the Wei Jin period provided the soil for Ji Kang's repeated and difficult writing style; on the other hand, Ji Kang's own genius thinking stood out among them and was not well corrected. The ideological beauty of metaphysics is perfectly reflected in Ji Kang's articles. If we learn about Ji Kang's life and lament and cry over what happened to him — especially his death — brought about a kind of emotional and spiritual purification, then we had the pleasure of thinking by wandering around Ji Kang's ideological world and participating in debates with him. What is even more valuable is that the author's pen is often emotional and has a strong sense of depression. It seems to have overflowed the boundaries of historians, yet it has restored the true face of history and people in history.
What is most impressive in “The Biography of Ji Kang” is Ji Kang's multiple conflicting ideological personality. Wang Xiaoyi's review can be described as accurate: “(Ji Kang) claims to be a believer in the old, naturally reckless ideology, and indeed pursues the realm of 'watching for Hong and wielding five strings', but he is unable to escape his sense of responsibility as Cao Wei's royal family, showing the almost paranoid Confucian spirit of 'knowing the impossible. '” At the same time, Ji Kang himself “taught by name and left to nature,” but in the “Book of Commandments,” which has the nature of a will, he taught his son vulgar survival tips and designed an exemplary lifestyle policy for his son. Detailed rules on language behavior, treatment of people, and even financial management can be seen from Ji Kang's insight into the rules of interpersonal communication and the subtlety of human nature. However, he didn't do that himself.
Precisely because of this kind of “heavenly punishment, easy to solve!” The contradiction is that when the Zen era had come to an end and the whole society surrendered to Sima, Ji Kang resolutely ended his career as a hermit, stepped forward, and agreed with his life. “Time for execution is over, Ji Kang calmly stretches his neck. The executioner's butcher took off and landed, and one head landed. Ji Kang was freed and completely freed. Everyone present will age, but Ji Kang will never get old. In the historical memory of generations, he will always be 39 years old.”
Reading Ji Kang's life, we'll find that he answered some of the fundamental questions of life: How do people grow and seek nature in the wasteland? How to choose between life and death? How can we hold on to some indestructible values in the midst of misery? He used his life, and especially blood and death, to give the answer. Reading Ji Kang's thoughts, we'll find Ji Kang full of curiosity: are public and private important or not? What does music have to do with people's emotions? Are there any advantages or disadvantages to housing, and will it affect people's lives? What is the core of wellness, and how do you understand desire? Various issues have not only reverberated in history, but are also closely related to modern people. The specific confusion of our time is still related to Ji Kang's concerns.
