 Mongolian mythology of creation talks about the creation of the world in this way: the beginning of the world was a vast ocean of water. Wazirbani (King Kong) found the white diving bird in the sky and ordered it to dive into the seabed to find dirt. Afterwards, he used this dirt to create the world with the power of his language and magic. Diving birds also dragged giant turtles from the seabed as a stronghold for the god of creation. [Mongolia\] Zeng Durham: “The Mythological Image of Mongolia” (Cyrillic Mongolian), Ulaanbaatar: National Press, 1989, p. 129. In other Mongolian mythology, it is also often said that Burhan Baghsi (Buddha) or Shakyamuni created the world by sprinkling a patch of earth on the ocean; then, plants and everything grew in the world, and eventually humans appeared. In Buryat Mongolian mythology, which preserves shamanic traditions, the role of Wazirbani Buddha or Shakyamuni is played by “Eh Burhan,” the mother god, an ancient shaman god.
This is the quintessential “myth of scuba diving for mud.” Mythologists have long pointed out that this myth is the myth that shamans created the world—the creator God used the power of words and spells to create the world. As can be seen, the origin of the Mongolian myth of creation is very ancient, but in many other texts, the influence of Buddhism is also very obvious. In Mongolian mythology, the origin of humans is almost always linked to the creation of the world. The Creator God created everything in the world after creating the world, and humans only appeared after plants and animals. This actually also suggests that humans were separated from animals. Corresponding to the ancient myths of diving influenced by Buddhism mentioned above, there are also myths about the origin of the world and myths about the fall of humans directly derived from Buddhist scriptures. For example, in some mythological texts, it is mentioned that the world was chaotic at the beginning, then the clean air rose and the turbidity sank, forming the world; humans were initially like gods, were able to fly freely, and their bodies glowed. Later, when they ate rice grown on the ground, they lost their light and the ability to fly, and the world became dark. Later, Buddha created the Sun and Moon, illuminated the world, and saved humanity. This myth was mainly introduced to the Mongolian region through Buddhism, and is included in almost all Mongolian Buddhist historical works.
Mongolian mythology of creation not only talks about the origin of the world and humans, but also many myths about the Sun, Moon, and the stars. The most famous ones include the myth that the Archer's forehead was black and Morgen shot the Sun, and the myth of the Milky Way and the Seven Stars of the North Dipper. There are many variants of the myth that the forehead is black and the morigan shoots the day. For example, some variants say that seven suns have appeared in the world, and there has been a drought; the archer Erikko Morrigan decided to shoot out seven suns and vowed, “If I don't shoot all seven suns, I'll cut off my thumbs and go into a cave without seeing the sun, and become a marmot.” Black Morigan shot down the sun one by one, but when it was about to shoot the 7th sun, the swallow blocked the sun. The black moregen on the forehead hit the swallow's tail, and the last sun survived. Other variants say that seven stars appeared in the sky, and the ground became extremely cold, so Black Morghan shot seven stars on the forehead. Whether it's the sun or the stars, whether it's dry or cold, Erikhi Morrigan is a tragic hero who brought the world back to normal. According to the myth of the Milky Way and the Seven Dipper stars, in ancient times, tens of thousands of armies rushed cows and sheep to the west, and walked to the sky. The path they walked was the Milky Way in the sky. At that time, Hohwadai Mohgen Khan also rushed with the army and hunted along the way, hunting countless prey. Samsung in the Orion constellation is said to be the three female deer being chased by Hohwadai Moguen, and there are also three fawns running with the doe, and behind them, Hohwadai Morigun with two hunting dogs is closely chasing after them. The Seven Stars of the Beidou are said to be Hohedai Morigen's horse chaining two horses, and seven elderly people guard Hohodai Morigen's two horses every night. This myth is well-known among Mongolians, and it graphically explains the origin of the Milky Way, Samsung, and the Seven Stars of the Beidou using the unique thinking of migratory nomadic and hunting cultures.
