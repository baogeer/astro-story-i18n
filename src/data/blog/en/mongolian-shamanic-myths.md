 Mongolians believed in shamanism in the early days, and there are many shamanic myths circulating in the Mongolian region. “The First Female Shaman” circulating in Mongolia says: Once upon a time, after Tenjin created humans, they sent eagles to protect humans so that humans would not be harmed by demons. Eagles wander around humans and do their part, but humans don't like vultures, so let children drive away the vultures. The eagle returned to heaven and reported what happened to Tenjin. Tenjin said to the eagle, “Then pass on your magic to humans and let humans protect themselves!” The eagle flew back to the world and saw a young girl who was herding sheep in the wild but lost her way sleeping under a tree. The eagle taught her his magic. The young girl woke up after falling asleep for three days. After returning home, she was harshly criticized by her brother. Suddenly, my brother got sick, and my sister said, “Let me cure your illness.” The girl made her brother lie down on the white felt and placed a wooden pillar with a branch under him. The brother immediately recovered from his illness. Since then, this young girl became the first female shaman. This myth reflects the shaman's healing functions and rituals. Ancient Mongolian shamans didn't use medicine to cure diseases, but instead held shamanic rituals to expel demons. When a person is sick, it is up to the shaman to declare that a god or demon is the cause of the illness, and what the god or devil wants from people — they mostly agree to replace the patient's soul with some kind of animal. As long as the shaman agrees, he takes this animal with him. He also has to perform various rituals and gestures to move the devil from the patient to his own body, and then from his own body to that animal.
Another Mongolian shaman myth says that eagles are divine messengers of Tenger Tenjin. It was ordered to descend to earth, marry a tribe leader, and give birth to a beautiful girl. Condor taught her the magic of being psychic with heaven and gods, and used her own feathers to knit a divine coat for the girl, and put a crown made of feathers on her head, allowing her to travel the world and nurture her into the world's earliest amazing female shaman. Most Mongolian shaman costumes have oversized cuffs, and the front and back flaps are covered with long and short cloth strips of various colors, which look like bird feathers. This shaman suit actually symbolizes a sacred bird like an eagle. Once the shaman wears it, he can fly to another world.
Among Mongolians and other northern peoples who believe in shamanism, shamans want to go to the underworld to save the souls of people taken away by demons to cure patients' illnesses. The reason for this is that since the world was formed, a struggle between gods and demons began. The result of the struggle was that demons would harm humans created by God, causing humans to face illness and death, while God sent shamans to save and heal humans. For example, in the Buryat epic “Gesr,” after killing the god Atai Ulan, the head of the evil god of the East, the god Khanholmusta Tengger dropped the body of the god Atai Ulan to the earth. His left hand, upper body, and lower body became three giant demons and harmed humans. Finally, Gesr destroyed them and saved humans. This mythological theme is actually also one of the core themes of Mongolian heroic epics — the hero goes to enemy territory and rescues stolen tribesmen — to a certain extent, the epic hero is also a shaman. It can be said that there is an intrinsic connection between Mongolian creation myths and shamanic myths, and at a stage of high cultural development, myths about the struggle between gods and demons were formed. The most famous of these is the myth of a struggle between the Forty-Four Evil Tenjin of the East and the 55 Good Tenjin of the West. These highly developed social myths later directly gave birth to Mongolian heroic epics such as “Gesr.”
