 Some people say, “War keeps women away.” Women have also always been viewed as weak on the battlefield. However, wars are cruel, and real wars don't really let women go away. In ancient and modern wars between China and foreign countries, you can always see them weak on battlefields full of sword, sword, or artillery fire. On the Waterloo battlefield in 1815, many women used their own flesh and blood to carry the bloodshed and carnage of the battlefield. Many of them ended up dying on the battlefield. However, women's bloodshed was very common throughout Napoleonic times. In this epic war, these women became the protagonists of many legends, and they witnessed the harshness and sorrow of the Battle of Waterloo.
French woman on the battlefield
On June 18, 1815, the Armageddon between Napoleon and the anti-French coalition officially began in Waterloo. On the Waterloo battlefield, you can often see dead women, but no one knows their former fame. After the war, Smith, a volunteer from the 95th Raid Regiment, discovered a beautiful girl who had been taken by the Grim Reaper on the battlefield and buried her there. And the only thing Smith knows -- this is a beautiful French girl, and she must have fallen silently on the battlefield during a fierce fight. As for who she is? Why did you come to the battlefield? They have all become mysteries that will never be solved.
This French girl wasn't the only woman to die in the Battle of Waterloo. The British army discovered the death of the two French girls in the middle of only one battle. Captain Henry Ross of the British 32nd Infantry Regiment later recalled, “I saw one of them. She was wearing a light yellow jacket and pants. Her head was pierced by a bullet”.
No one knows exactly how many French women followed their loved ones to the battlefield, or even ended up sleeping there forever. But it was no accident that they appeared on the battlefield. When Marshal Massena invaded Portugal in 1810, his wife also wore a cavalry uniform and followed him as his lieutenant. Unfortunately, the appearance of Madame Massena caused much trouble for the French army led by Massena. Not only did she distract Massena, but she also had many conflicts with Massena's subordinates, causing the military to lose heart and slow to act. After the war, Massena also admitted, “My biggest mistake was to bring women to the battlefield”. But throughout the Napoleonic era, Mrs. Massena wasn't the only woman who followed her husband to the front line.
In the French army, every regiment allows women to join the army on the battlefield. They also wear military uniforms (at least some of them) and work mainly as health workers or logistics workers. Their main job in the troupe is selling or nursing. They used trolleys to sell cigarettes, drinks, and liquor to fighters, or take care of the wounded. As the war progresses and the front line progresses, some women inevitably have to cross the line of fire, and some are injured or killed as a result. Mary Bowes, a caregiver in the First Bomb Detachment of the Napoleonic Guards, fell near the point where a shell landed during the Battle of Waterloo.
The British Army's “Family Army”
In the British military, although there are no women such as caregivers, they have female family members associated with the military. As can often be seen, these female military families braved the onslaught of bullets to rush to the front line. Many soldiers are already married, but the British army at the time only allowed four to six soldiers per company to carry female family members to serve with the military. Once the regiment is sent to the front line, more female families will join the army in violation of regulations, and a huge, unofficial “family army” will immediately form on the front line.
On June 16, 1815, many families of British military officers were attending a dance party hosted by Duchess Richard in Brussels. Napoleon had just issued an order to invade Belgium at the time. The news from France caused great chaos and panic at the dance party. Mrs. Georgiana recalled, “It was a horrible night. After saying goodbye to my friends that night, many never met again.”
Miss Charlotte Ann is an Englishman living in Brussels. She recalled the sad scene the next morning: “It was a miserable parting scene. The men were forced to leave their wives and children, and this was probably the last time they met. Heartbreak tears flashed on the faces of many soldiers. A poor man, just under my window, ran back again and again and reluctantly said goodbye to his wife; he held the child tightly in his arms and couldn't bear to let go. When he put down his kids for the last time, he resolutely wiped away his tears with his sleeve and quickly rushed back to his team. Troops have begun to assemble on the other side of Plaça Reial. Many of the soldier's wives even followed their husbands to the assembly site. I saw a young English lady riding a horse, silently following her husband all the way out of town...”
And most of the women who followed the Duke of Wellington to the Waterloo front inevitably fell victim to the war. A sergeant of the 28th Infantry Regiment was wounded, and his wife, who accompanied him, unfortunately fell due to a terrible shell explosion while escorting him out of the battlefield. When cleaning up the battlefield after the war, people discovered the bodies of many female British military families, and even a mother and son were swallowed up by shell explosions at the same time.
When the fierce battle was over, many women began searching for the bodies of their loved ones in the dead mountains and seas of blood. In extreme pain, some of them are even mechanically numb to bandage the wounds of their dead loved ones, while others kiss their loved ones' remains as a final goodbye. Perhaps they are also saying goodbye to the joy of heaven and joy that they have passed away with their loved ones. After all the corpses have been properly buried, many British women who have lost loved ones are still wandering around the battlefield sad, in grief, and in some cases, to the point of insanity. Perhaps women who haven't left the battlefield for a long time just want to hear the laughter and laughter of their loved ones in the past, and the melodious song that God would also shed tears...
Being away is also painful
Those women who did not go to the battlefield with their loved ones are suffering even more mentally, even though they have less pain on the battlefield.
Military wives and women left behind in Brussels, including the northernmost port of Antwerp, have endured cruel mental torture while waiting uneasy. After learning the news of her husband's death, a military officer's wife suddenly changed her face. While wandering aimlessly around the square, she screamed hysterically: “My husband is not dead! He'll be back soon...” The women left behind not only worked hard for the lives and deaths of their loved ones, but were also concerned about the war situation ahead. “What exactly is going on with their army on the battlefield? Did the campaign go well?” This kind of question has also haunted their minds for a long time. Of course, their concerns are justified; if the French army captures Brussels, they will face humiliation by French soldiers.
Mrs. Magdalene Lance is the epitome of those anxiously waiting in the back for her husband to return safely from the battlefield. On April 4, 1815, the beautiful young Magdalene married Colonel William Howe Lance, the Duke of Wellington's Deputy General Commander. However, when war was about to break out, Magdalene moved to the rear to take refuge from the flames of war. There, rumors spread from time to time that William had died or was still alive, leaving her fearful and mentally devastated by her husband all day long. Eventually, she learned that her husband was still alive, but he was seriously injured. She immediately rushed to her husband and accompanied him through the last days of his life. On June 26, William died due to severe injuries, and at this point they had been married for less than three months. Magdalene Lance later wrote the very famous autobiographical novel “A Week in Waterloo” based on her own personal experience. After reading her work, the famous English novelist Charles Dickens commented with emotion: “If I could live 50 years from now until I die, I would definitely often dream about those real scenes.”
Survivor's Memories
Most of the English women who visited Waterloo have disappeared from the memories of future generations, yet a few of them will always be cherished by survivors of some wars. After the Battle of Waterloo broke out, the wife of Alexander Rose, the 14th Infantry Regiment, remained on the front line with her husband. Her friend urged her to leave the battlefield, but she was unwilling to die. She hoped to stay on the battlefield and fight side by side with her husband to help care for the wounded. But in the end, she succumbed to her husband's pleas and retreated to a church bell tower to take refuge. There, she truly witnessed the bloodthirsty nature of war and the weakness and strength of women on the battlefield...
Another recorded woman who participated in the Battle of Waterloo was 26 year old Jenny Jones. On her tombstone in Wales, she wrote: “She is the wife of a soldier of the 23rd Royal Welch Fusiliers. She fought side by side with her husband on the Waterloo front for three days.”
Some of the female survivors of the Battle of Waterloo have even lived to a large age.
