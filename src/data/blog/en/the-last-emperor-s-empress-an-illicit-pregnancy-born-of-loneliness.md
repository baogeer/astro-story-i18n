 This article is an excerpt from “The First Half of My Life”, author: Aixinjue Luo Puyi, Publishing House: People's Press
I have had four wives. According to the saying at the time, they were one queen, one concubine, and two nobles. If in essence, none of them are my wives; I don't have a wife at all; all I have is furnishings, furnishings to solve different problems. Although their specific experiences are different, they are all victims of the same system.
For a long period of time, she was treated with indifference and annoyance. Her experience was probably the most incomprehensible for young people in modern new China. Had her future fate not been decided as soon as her family was born, her fate would have been arranged as soon as she got married. I used to think later that if she were able to get divorced from me like Wenxiu when she was in Tianjin, it probably wouldn't have ended like that. Of course, she's different from Wenxiu after all. In Wenxiu's thoughts, there is one thing that is more valued than feudal status and rituals. This is the idea that demands freedom and the family life of an ordinary person. However, in Wan Rong's mind, she values the status of a “queen” even more. She would rather be a famous wife than lose her status as a “queen.” Even though she suddenly thought about getting a divorce, her situation was different from Wen Xiu. Wen Xiu was able to find some support from her family and friends, and Wan Rong's father, brother, and master not only wouldn't support her, but I'm afraid they would have to stop her or even put pressure on her.
Since she pushed Wen Xiu away, I have become disgusted with her. I rarely talk to her and don't pay much attention to her affairs, so I haven't heard her talk about her feelings, pain, and wishes. What happened later showed that she was actually an individual and had the normal needs of the average person. She had a very peculiar mentality. On the one hand, she had normal needs, and on the other hand, she refused or couldn't give up the title of empress to establish a reasonable life. As a result, she engaged in fornication, and also became addicted to drug addiction (opiates).
No matter what, she can't be responsible for this kind of thing; at least she shouldn't be responsible for all of it herself. In fact, I placed all the responsibility on her at the time; I never blamed myself at all, let alone the system of blaming people for eating people.
In fact, her drug use was due to ideas given by her parents and even encouraged by her brother (who is dead) when it comes to fornication. I didn't know until very late that when she left Tianjin for Dalian, her brother sold his younger sister to a Japanese military officer in exchange for some kind of profit.
I discovered the problem in 1935 because she was pregnant and about to give birth. My feelings at the time were hard to describe. I was angry and didn't want Japanese people to know; the only way was to vent my anger on her. In addition to evicting all those involved with her and those suspected, I also decided to divorce her. In my words at the time, I “abandoned” her. Since the Japanese, who was the deputy head of the Imperial Household, and the Kwantung Army did not allow it, I was afraid to offend the Japanese, so I made another deliberate move to show Wan Rong, that is, choose another “noble person.”
Wan Rong may have had a dream until her death, dreaming that her child is still alive. She didn't know that the child would be burnt in a boiler for the rest of her life; she only knew that his older brother raised the child for her, and that her brother had to take a monthly childcare allowance from her. Original editor's note: Wan Rong's newborn baby was thrown into a boiler for incineration, and the final version was abridged.
When she broke up with me after “August 15,” she was very addicted to smoking and became very ill. She died of illness in Jilin the following year.
