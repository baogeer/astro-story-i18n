 The earliest recorded legend of the Bamboo King is the Eastern Jin historical book “Huayang Guo Zhi”: “Those who have King Bamboo are happy to be born out of water. One woman was at the waterfront, and there were three large bamboo shoots flowing into the women's footbed. They refused to go. They heard someone's voice, took it, and broke it. So Xiongyi, the family took bamboo as the surname, and donated it to the field to become a bamboo forest.” Then, starting with Fan Ye's “Book from the Later Han Dynasty,” Zhu Wang was also combined with Ye Lang.
Ye Lang Ze, a woman was washing in the dushui (present-day Beipanjiang River in western Guizhou). A large three-section long bamboo tube drifted between the woman's feet and refused to drift away. I heard a child crying in the tube. Cut open a bamboo tube, see a baby boy, take him back and raise him. He is talented and has martial arts. He stands on his own as Lord Ye Lang, and takes Bamboo as his last name. Where broken bamboo tubes are abandoned, lush bamboo groves grow. Later generations built the “Chikuo-ji Temple” for worship.
Since then, the “Bamboo King Legend Circle” has become a paradise for people to enjoy bamboo in harmony. People who have lived here for generations have created a wide variety of beautiful and practical bamboo products, interpreting a variety of legends about Bamboo King and Ancient Yorang. They have also created religious bamboo totem worship, a nation that “takes bamboo as its surname”, diverse bamboo folk customs, and the most typical bamboo divination. They are living fossils with a long history of Chinese bamboo culture, and they are also a vast and profound concentrated version.
Since then, the “Bamboo King Legend Circle” has become a hot spot for bamboo research. Foresters say this is the “main place of origin” and “distribution center” of bamboo in the world or Asia; archaeologists say it has the largest number of bamboo fossils unearthed in China; historians say this is probably the place where descendants of the Guzhu Nation moved in; folklorists say it is a “living fossil” of Chinese folk culture.
