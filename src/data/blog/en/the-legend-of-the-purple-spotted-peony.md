 At the end of the Ming dynasty, there was an impoverished person with extensive knowledge, and was good at piano, chess, calligraphy, and painting. He only saw through the “red dust,” refused to take refuge from the world, shaved his hair as a monk, and lived in seclusion at Baiyun Temple in Taibai Mountain under the name “Shi Yi Shou.”
Yamadera is surrounded by mountains and water. The scenery is beautiful, and peonies are planted widely in the courtyard. In monasteries, with the exception of practicing Buddhism, Yishou almost always studies ink and paints in his spare time. He is particularly good at painting peonies. His paintings are delicate and brushwork, just like nature. Everyone in the audience clapped their hands.
The fame of Yi Shou's paintings soon spread far and wide, and there was an endless flow of painters.
One spring day, around the valley rain, peonies compete to attract good men and women from all sides to visit Chaoshan to worship Buddha and enjoy the flowers in order to be rich, lucky, and healthy.
On the afternoon of this day, Yi Shou was painting peonies in the courtyard, ignoring the loud voices in front of the hospital. Looking up, there were a few people dressed up in Zhuang Ding in the distance, crowding around a “fat man” who looked precious, and waved over here. Walking nearby, it became clear that the fat man was the famous local bully “Wang Dahan”. This person has always been rampant in the village, oppressing the people, and doing nothing wrong. “Wang Dahan” walked up to Yi Shou and saw that he was a good painter and salivated. When he was in a hurry to get it, he instigated Zhuang Ding to ask for it. What kind of personality did Yi Shou have? How could he join forces with “Wang Dayi,” and now he refused. “Wang Dahan” was annoyed and forced to take a picture. Yi Shou showed no sign of weakness, tore it up, threw the brush into the inkstone, and went away angrily. “Wang Da-yu” saw that the onlookers were outraged and had no choice but to remain quiet.
Who knows, the ink that spilled out of the inkstone fell on the petals of a few nearby peonies, and also flowed down the petal to the base of the petal, condensing into purple spots.
Since then, every year during the blooming season, people can clearly see the purple spots on the flowers, so they are called “purple-spotted peonies.”
(Quoted from “National Flower Festival” by Liu Xiang and Xu Xiaofan)
