 Legend has it that a long time ago, a group of the Pu people, the ancestors of the Yilao tribe in Wuchuan, Guizhou, split rocks deep in the mountains. One of the young men discovered that red grains of sand were shining in the excavated stones and took them home curiously. The mountain goods merchant came to the banks of the Hongdu River, heard that red stones had been found here, and found a young man storing the red stones. The merchant took a look at the stones and determined that it was cinnabar. They then carefully investigated and discovered the history of the red stones, and bought all the red stones. “Red stone is cinnabar. You can sell it for money. It's an auspicious thing, good luck!” Since then, there have been more Pu people mining Dansha. The lives of the Pu people have improved since then, and cinnabar became the earliest tribute made by the Pu people to the Western Zhou dynasty. In order to appease people's hearts, the emperor discovered that the young man who discovered cinnabar was “King Bao.” The channel connecting Mukawa to the outside world was opened by cinnabar, from the Hongdu River, through the Wujiang River to the Yangtze River. Nakahara culture was also introduced to Mukawa via waterways. Pu people also learned how to extract mercury from cinnabar, and their fame spread far and wide. To commemorate “Bao Wang,” future generations built “Baowang Temple” one after another in places such as Sankeng, Banchang, and Yinqian Gou, which are rich in cinnabar, to enjoy sacrifices. Just today, villagers in the Daping area must pay homage to “Bao Wang Bodhisattva,” light incense for him, drink and meat, meditate on their words, point their fingers red on their foreheads, and pray for blessings. In the sand cave where something unlucky happened, you also need to ask the gentleman to draw a mantra. The charm is stamped with cinnabar and affixed to the entrance of the sand cave.
