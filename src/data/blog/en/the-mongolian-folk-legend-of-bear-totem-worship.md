 “Blue Wolf and White Deer” is clearly recorded in “The Secret History of Mongolia” as only a beggar. Bao Er is only the founder of the Jin clan, so it does not represent an ancestral totem from all Mongolian departments. In the vast North, there are many other tribes that live and thrive on this land, and they all have totems they worship. For example, Buryats and Dalhatians worship bears. Buryatians and Dalhatians often call bears “dbege” (ancestor), “qairqan” (sacred), and “otog” (another name for bears), and regard them as the king of ferocious beasts. When hunting bears, they have to follow the “bear rituals” that have been handed down since ancient times and hold many unusual sacrificial ceremonies. According to legend, there is a story like this: “A woman met a bear and gradually became familiar. Later, this woman gave birth to several children similar to bears. After raising the children, she went back to the bear. Before leaving, I left a message: 'Don't kill bears for three years; doing that would kill me'. But the children still disobeyed their mother's warning and killed a bear on the head in the third year. When the bear's belly was cut open, they saw the woman's breast inside”.
Bear worship exists not only in Buryatia, but there are similar phenomena in other regions. While traveling in southern Mongolia in the late 19th century, Russian scholars and geographers Bo and Kazarov recorded a story of Mongols in the Qaidam Basin using bears as their ancestors: there is a young woman named “Huiteng.” One day, she entered Xia Saiiri Mountain, which was infested with wild beasts. While she was wandering around in fear of entering a desperate situation, a green beast quietly appeared near her. She was taken aback and frightened when she saw it. The beast felt sorry for this frightened woman, brought her food, and cared for her kindly. Soon, they became attached to each other, lived together, and had a bear. Mongols and Tibetans in Qaidam worship bears and call them “tengu.”
According to research, there are many bear worship ruins in the northern hemisphere, and there are many remains related to bear worship in North Asia near Buryatia, including “bear rituals” that can be seen in the public and customs related to bears. Dalhart people follow a very special set of customs and manners when hunting bears; for example, male bears can hunt all year round, but not female bears; they don't start hunting until they have given birth to cubs and walk out of their caves in spring. When hunting bears in a cave, you must first pray earnestly near the entrance of the cave, knock on the sickle, and throw fire at the entrance three times. After catching a bear, the bear's head cannot be peeled for a certain period of time. The head and two front legs are placed in a specific position, and the skin can only be peeled and cooked after sacrificing it. At the time of tasting, starting with the oldest person in the village, let everyone eat the bear head meat in turn, then take the bear skull to the wild and hang it on the tree. This kind of custom of completing a series of “bear rituals” is not unique to Buryats and Dalhatians. According to research, the Karelians and Leami people in ancient Finland in Europe, and the Evinki (Tungus) people, Ukagir people, Orunchun people, and Ewenke people in Asia all had similar bear worship customs.
Judging from the data presented above, although it is a remnant of bear worship ruins, it reflects a very ancient concept of totems. For example, calling bears a grandfather, father, or ancestor directly is a sign that people regard bears as being related by blood, so they call them by corresponding family titles and treat them like relatives. This is a reflection of the Totem family concept, and it also originated in the early days of totem culture. There are two myths and legends about bears intersecting with women. On the one hand, they indicate that people have a blood relationship with bears. For example, when bears give birth to a few bears, there are clouds of women's breasts in their abdominal cavity, reflecting the ancients hoping to be as ferocious as bears, or even fantasizing about becoming bears, showing the characteristics of humans “fear created gods” in the early days. On the other hand, the women in the two myths are the protagonists of the story, and may also be related to the religious life of matrilineal clan societies. Mongols in Qaidam call bears “tengu”, which is puzzling, because “tengu” and “Sirius” are actually names of the same nature, so why are bears and wolves mixed together? Perhaps Mongolians here worshiped wolf totems before bear worship, so the original totem images were transferred to bears, so it can be inferred that the bear worship of the Qaidam people is probably a later occurrence.
The practice of both worshipping bears and hunting bears exists in many bear-worshiping nations. In ancient times, if an animal was used as a totem, the prohibition of killing and fasting was very strict. However, with the evolution of the times, changes in understanding, and the increase in population, in particular, animals such as bears are widespread, and their use value is high. If you follow the ancient rules, life will be extremely difficult, so the taboo on bears gradually loosened, and the totem taboo evolved. This is the beginning of bear hunting and eating bears. The Buryats did this, and almost all the other mountain forest peoples of the North did the same. The remedy is the implementation of the bear festival described above. Sharing bear head meat seems to be a remnant of the ancient primitive “totem communion” custom. Freud thought, “The totem meal is probably the earliest human celebration ceremony...” Stokarev also pointed out that “totem communion” is the original form of the widely popular “communion” ceremony. According to primitive people, using the blood and flesh of the totem as communion would further strengthen their close relationship with the totem. Regain the Totem's Spirit Power. For this reason, some ethnic groups also have a custom of celebrating the Bear Festival. The Gilyaks in Siberia worship bears and hold a festival where they kill bears and share bear meat once a year. It can be seen that similar customs are all related to religious activities such as totem worship.
