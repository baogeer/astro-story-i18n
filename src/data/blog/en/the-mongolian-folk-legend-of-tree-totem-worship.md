 Generally speaking, there are many plant totems in the south, and rare in the north. If you look at a wide range of plant varieties, there is some truth to this statement. However, judging from Mongolian tree worship alone, the tree worship concept left over in myths and legends or Mongolian heroic epics is quite representative in plant worship. Hunters of all ethnic groups in the north have lived in mountains and forests since ancient times, nurtured and developed. The Mongols also began to use forests as cradles, and then advanced towards grasslands. Mongolians have a very ancient worship of trees. For example, there is a myth that their ancestors came from trees in the Chulus Department, one of the four weirats in Western Mongolia. Books such as Batur and Ubash Tumen's “The History of the Four Weirats”, the book of the same name by Gaban Shehrib, and “The Baby Rosary” by Galaden record this myth and legend. In 1982, “The Origin of Rus Dzhungar” in “Mongolian Historical Legends” by Baowin Hershige was published and published. The idea is: Legend has it that in ancient times, there were two families named Amine and Tumenei who lived and reproduced deep in the mountains and old forests. One of their descendants is an expert hunter. One day, this hunter found a large tree in the forest, with a tumor in the middle, and a baby lying in the cavity (hollow). The upper end of the tree tumor has a branch fork shaped like a leaky tube. The tip fits right into the baby's mouth. The tree juice enters the baby's body through the leak tube and becomes his food. The tree is carefully guarded by a scabbard. The hunter then took the baby back and raised him, calling the baby “the nephew of Tenjin (Tengri), whose mother is a tree baby and a father.” When the baby became an adult, he was promoted as the leader, and his children and grandchildren grew into the Russian tribe.” Called “Ross”, the meaning of nurturing people through tree branches. There are also statements such as referring to people with babies and their descendants as the “Junggar” ministry.
Mythological stories similar to those born in trees have spread widely among the Altai-speaking peoples. Judging from various Mongolian ministries, the Chulus tribe of Kalmyk Mongols also believes that their ancestor was a “Willow Baodong (Hercules) master whose father was an exquisite tree and an owl was the mother...” The surname “Ilgai” (larch) was also found in the lineage of the Durbert department. The last sentence of the “Ancestors of Swans, Birch Tree Pole” sung by Buryat shamans explains that the swan mother was tied to a birch stump, so the birth of their ancestors was related to birds and trees. Mongolians' worship of trees and sacrifices are clearly expressed and recorded in books such as “The Secret History of Mongolia” and in shamans' rituals. The origin of customs such as one-tree trees, lush trees, “shaman trees”, birches, and larch trees is all related to the idea of tree totems. The myths and legends from which this idea originated are spread throughout the Manyitongus language family; the Turkic language family. This kind of story of leaders and ancestors, born from a combination of trees, birds, and cradles, records the legend that Uighurs gave birth to five children in “The History of Dusan Mongolia”. “There are five chambers, a kind of tent, and a silver net hanging on each net, and a baby is sitting in it.” This is very similar to Galadon's “Baby Rosary” describing the baby as being born by a celestial maiden and hanging from a tree.” “Silver Net” is the cradle, and the “tree power leak tube” is breast milk. Naturally, the growth of a baby is inseparable from these two “babies”. Our ancestors thought that trees also had this instinct. This is a reflection of the idea that they were unable to distinguish between people and the natural world and confuse social groups with animals and plants in nature. Engels said, “People have received support from other entities in their development, but these entities, not angels, but lower-level entities, are animals, and this has given rise to animal worship...” Plants can be considered the lowest level of natural entities. The collection era was inseparable from all kinds of plants to nurture humans. Primitive hunters living in alpine jungles were inseparable from wild animals and birds, as well as the blessings brought by forests. Birch sap is said to be a good nutrient beneficial to human growth. The northern willow trees are very rich, and the bark can be made into various tools. There are also green pine and green cypresses that are evergreen all year round. They are majestic, upright, and spectacular. In the eyes of the ancients, they were most amazing. They not only think they are blessed by trees, but they also think that trees are mysterious, friendly, and respectable. Appearance, consciousness, and emotion are mixed and blended, and fantasy and reality are indistinguishable, so they regard trees as having a blood relationship with themselves, so they respect and worship them. This is the origin of the concept of tree totems in the ancient northern mountains and forest peoples.
The above describes seven types of totem myths: wolves, deer, bears, cows, swans, eagles, and trees. These myths not only reflect the unique ethnic characteristics of Mongolian totem mythology, but also have many things in common with the myths of the northern peoples, especially those in the Altaic language family. The reason for this is that they are related to roughly the same regional environment and social life, and at the same time are closely linked to cultural integration. Various ethnic groups in the Altai language family have had many interlaced migrations, contacts, wars, or annexations in history, thus forming a “cultural circle” with the same attributes. Myths with the same theme often take on new forms by passing down from generation to generation, and mutating or confusing with each other in combination with the characteristics of the family. Therefore, this section (including the following sections), which shows the mythology of the ancient Mongolian society, should also be grasped from the regional environment, economic form, social life, religious beliefs, and historical changes of the entire northern people, so as to make an accurate analysis and evaluation.
