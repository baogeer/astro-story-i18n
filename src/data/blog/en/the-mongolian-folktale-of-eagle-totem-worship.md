 The eagle (Boujgud) is a divine symbol of the incarnation of a shaman. Explaining the origin of Mongolian shamans is inseparable from eagles. The traditional shaman saying, “The eagle is the god bird messenger from the sky. It was ordered to marry the head of the tribe and give birth to a beautiful girl, and the condor taught her the magic of heaven and gods, and used its own feathers to weave the girl a divine coat. A crown made of feathers was placed on her head to invite her to the celestial world, and nurtured her into an amazing 'udugan' (udugan)”. This is a myth explaining the origin of the Mongolian witch. The earliest incarnation of a male shaman is also related to an eagle. Former Soviet Hangarov's “Anthology” records this legend: “At the beginning of the world, there were no diseases or deaths. Soon after, demons spilled sickness and death on the earth, and people began to suffer. At this point, the gods sent eagles from heaven to help each other. However, this specially sent eagle had a hard time falling to the ground. People on the ground could neither understand its words, nor could they understand why it came to earth? As a last resort, the eagle flew at the feet of the gods and returned to the sky. As a result, the gods ordered the eagle to say, “Once on earth, teach the shaman's skills to the first person you meet. Thus, once again, the eagle came to the world and saw a woman sleeping under a tree at first sight. The eagle intersected with this woman and made her pregnant. At this point, this woman was temporarily separated from her husband, waiting for her to return to her husband and give birth to a boy at full term. This is the earliest shaman in the world.
The former is an ominous source of the two sources mentioned above; the latter is a Shaman myth and legend belonging to Buryatia. Similar myths that are commonly spread can be seen from Europeans' travel records. Eagles, birds of prey, have been admired and admired by people since the early days of hunting. Their superb flying skills, ferocious and unusual grappling skills, and proud standing and majesty make people feel amazing and admirable. Although it does not pose a threat to humans, in the face of harsh natural environments, hunting ancestors fantasized that they had the same skills as eagles, which was extremely natural. Due to the development of the hunting industry, hunters domesticated eagles as their assistants, and harriers became their indispensable support. Therefore, the flying Hawk Harrier and the Mercedes-Benz reindeer can be called the hunters' left and right legs. Hunters went from being secretly envious to relying on friendship, thus establishing an extremely close relationship with eagles. This is the reason why eagles have become gods widely believed in many nations. Judging from the worship of birds by all ethnic groups around the world, eagles account for a large proportion. The same is true of all ethnic groups in northern China. For example, the Manchu, Hezhe, Ewenke, and Kazaks all worship condors. In the Tohoku region in particular, almost every nation that believes in shamanism has its own eagle myths and legends, as well as various taboos and rituals for eagles.
Since shamans were conceived by condors, the eagle became a symbol of the original shaman spirit. In addition to installing antler ornaments on the shaman hat as a mana symbol, the bronze flying bird is the symbol of a condor. The Buryats revered the eagle as the protector of shamans, and they often chanted “Father of the Condor, the mother of Haddon the Phoenix” in their praises. Poems like this express their extraordinary origins and origins. It can be seen that the eagle is a unique sacred bird in the Holy Family of Shamans, and is also a symbol of good fortune for northern hunters and nomads.
