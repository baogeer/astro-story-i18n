 Wolves raised young children and became great people. In addition to records in the history of northern peoples, Mongolian folk also have legends about werewolves. The story is as follows: Once upon a time, a group of hunters were hunting on the banks of the Clulun River and discovered that a female wolf led a 3- to 4-year-old boy in the wilderness. The hunters chased away the wolf and brought back the boy. They didn't know who he was born to, so they named him “Sharu.” With his ability to speak, Shalu was able to understand all kinds of animal languages; and he was drafted into the military and fought with Genghis Khan.
Once camping, when Sharu heard the howling of the wolf, he told the boss that there was a flood and that they had to set up camp again. Sure enough, the night was full of wind and rain, and the original camp was flooded. Since then, whenever people camp at night, the leader asks Sharu, he knows what is good and bad. Judging from the above historical records and folklore legends about werewolves, it is obvious that Mongolians have the idea that wolf totems are worshiped.
The transition from wife and husband to husband, and the birth of the concept of “child” (that is, “breed”) and “father,” couple marriage also transitioned into monogamous marriage customs. While men controlled their wives and children, they also controlled and occupied increasingly accumulated wealth. A strict patrilineal family system and the concept of inheritance of transferring property to children and grandchildren came into being. Since then, the status of women has plummeted, and an era of male-centered patriarchy has begun. Men became the protagonists of the historical stage, showing the power of male pioneers, so male heroes (mostly appearing as tribal leaders or chiefs) in war and organizational production were worshipped. Heroic mythology became the main genre during this period, and Mongolian mythology entered a period of prosperity. The creation myth of the worship of Tenjin and the conquest of nature, and some genealogical myths and legends were formed at this time. For example, the “99 Days” statement is a reflection of patriarchal social ideology. The Tenjin faith comes from the animal husbandry industry's dependence on and worship of celestial bodies. It has a development process from simple to complex, from low to advanced, and from deification to humanization. By the time of the 99 days of shamanism and legends, the Lord God in the Pantheon had already taken the throne, so Genghis Khan was also the son of Hormutha (Immortal, Emperor), who was born by fate. In historical books, these ancient myths and legends all give terms such as “destiny,” “God's will,” and “gift of heaven.” Obviously, this is a recognition of a combination of the idea of paternal lineage inheritance in the genealogy of gods.
In folklore myths and legends, male gods who conquered nature were promoted. These gods were no longer pure slaves of nature, but heroes who fought against heaven and earth and dared to contend with the evil forces of nature and society. The penetration of social content and the development of human nature are the main characteristics of the mythology of this period. Compared to the “Battle of Ice and Sky,” the hero shooting at the Sun, and the conquest of the Black Dragon King, etc., they all show the power of entry (gods) to conquer nature, overcome nature, and fearless heroism. As natural forces are gradually recognized, people's sense of subjectivity has been sublimated. Outstanding members of those clan groups, such as laborers and wise messengers who protect the safety of humans and animals, have been promoted to gods after death and are loved and worshiped by people. The livestock guardians “Jiyaki” and “Homura” are such mythical works.
Mongolian myths and legends did not have clear boundaries in ancient times. For example, the ancestral legend of the Begging Ministry recorded in (The Secret History of Mongolia) actually contains images of the remains of totem mythology, as well as vivid representations of the mythology of nurturing a child (God, being born with light). Since generations of Mongolian ancestors are familiar with memorizing the tradition of reciting their own genealogy, legends (actually myths) of the origin of the ancestors, which began in the totem era, have been passed down from generation to generation; in their ideas, it is true that they think this is the history of their ancestors. However, judging from the prescriptive nature of mythology, some of these ancient legends of so-called lineage genealogy are products of early primitive human thought stages. They are myths, not legends. Strictly speaking, the transition from myth to legend has its own development process. Only when mythology evolves into humanization and real people are elevated to stories created by God can it be legendary. Works that reflect the idea of totem worship and nature worship are generally difficult to call them historical legends. Therefore, after Mongolian legends were nurtured and developed together with early mythology, the later they showed their characteristics. In particular, after the establishment of the patriarchy system and the emergence of class struggles, some legendary heroic myths and legends were mostly real-life people with magical abilities. The stories also became closer to real life, clearly showing some characteristics of legends, and there were more and more works like this later. By the time the mythical era with permanent appeal became a thing of the past, legends continued to develop along their own path, and fantastic stories continued to flourish in the vast world of folk oral literature.
