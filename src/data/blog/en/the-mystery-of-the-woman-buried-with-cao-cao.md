 2010 is the “Three Kingdoms” year. The archeology of Cao Cao's tomb, which was hyped up before the Spring Festival, seemed like a prelude to the spectacular debut of the new “Three Kingdoms.” Now that the new “Three Kingdoms” is already on the air, attention to the archaeology of Cao Cao's tomb has quietly cooled down. In fact, experts have only just begun to get busy after all the media coverage. Not long ago, at the “Cao Cao Gaoling Archaeological Discoveries” academic symposium held in Anyang, the presidents of the Chinese Society of Qin and Han History and the Chinese Weijin North and South Dynasties History Society had in-depth discussions on several unresolved issues. According to Professor Hu Axiang, who attended the conference, an opinion of Liu Chi, a researcher at the Institute of History of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, attracted everyone's attention.
Is the male skull in Tomb No. 2 Cao Cao
According to materials issued by the Henan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology, Tomb No. 2, which has been cleared so far, is large-scale, with a total length of nearly 60 meters. The shape and structure of the brick tomb is similar to known high-grade tombs from Han and Wei, indicating that the owner's identity is high. The objects unearthed in the tomb have characteristics from the late Eastern Han period and match the year of Cao Caosheng's death.
The one that best shows the identity of the tomb's owner is an engraved stone plaque unearthed in the tomb, which says “The Great Tiger Sparrow Used by Wei Wu Wang Chang.” During the excavation, the public security authorities also recovered from tomb raiders a “Tiger Sword Used by Wei Wu Wang Chang” stone plaque and a “comfort stone used by Wei Wu Wang Chang” stone pillow stolen from Tomb No. 2. These accurate stone inscriptions suggest that the tomb's owner should have been “King Wei Wu” Cao Cao.
According to documentary records, before his birth, Cao Cao was first crowned “Wei Gong,” and later became “King Wei,” and after his death, he was nicknamed “King Wu.” His son, Cao Pi, called him the Empress, and honored him as “Emperor Wu”. Historically, he is known as “Emperor Wei Wu.” The stone plaque and stone pillow are inscribed as “King Wei Wu,” which is in line with the title when Cao Cao was buried.
Professor Hu Axiang also added evidence on this issue: in the late Eastern Han Dynasty and the beginning of the Three Kingdoms, the only person with the nickname “King Wei Wu” was Cao Cao; “Xigaoxue Village” and “Donggaoxue Village” are also quite consistent with Cao Cao's “Gao Ling” name recorded in historical documents.
In addition to the artifacts unearthed in the tomb, several human bones were also discovered in Tomb No. 2. According to archaeological experts, these bones belong to three individuals. One of them is a man around the age of 60, and the other two belong to two women. The man is around 60 years old, which is the same age as Cao Cao when he died. Two women, according to preliminary expert assessment, one is around 50 years old and the other is around 20 years old. However, at this academic conference held not long ago, it was this elderly woman's age that raised doubts among scholars about her identity.
Who do the two female skulls belong to
Researcher Liu Chi, who attended this academic conference, told the reporter, “The reason why there are different opinions is because there are many Mrs. Cao Cao. However, according to previous findings, some people think that the older woman in the tomb was probably Mrs. Ding, Cao Cao's former partner; the younger one was probably Mrs. Liu later. There is an obvious flaw in this assumption. This is because although Mrs. Ding was originally married to Cao Cao, she had no children and was abolished as early as the first year of Jian'an, it is unlikely that she and Cao Cao were buried together. It is true that Mrs. Liu died at a young age, but she gave birth to Cao Cao two children and a daughter; it is unlikely that she was only around 20 years old. Instead, Mrs. Bian, the mother of Emperor Wei Wen Cao Pi, was canonized as queen the year before Cao Cao died. According to the system at the time, it is most likely that she and Cao Cao will be buried together.”
Since Mrs. Bian and Cao Cao are most likely to be buried together, why do some experts think that the older woman in the tomb is Mrs. Ding? Turns out, this is related to Mrs. Bian's age. According to historical records, Mrs. Bian was born in 160 AD and died in 230 AD, 10 years after Cao Cao's death. Mrs. Bian is 70 years old, which is obviously quite different from the age of the older women in the tomb. It was on this basis that experts put forward different opinions.
These different opinions make the identity of the older woman in the tomb seem mysterious and unpredictable. She really should have been Mrs. Bian, but the hard evidence is there. The deceased was only around 50 years old, so she had nothing to do with Mrs. Bian in any way.
This woman around the age of 50 is not Mrs. Ding, nor Mrs. Liu, let alone Mrs. Bian. Who would she be? In other words, who is eligible to be buried with Wei Wu, Wang and Cao Cao? And that woman in her 20s, who is she?
Teeth may reveal the true identity of the owner
The academic conference held in Anyang gave Liu Chi an opportunity to answer questions.
“At an archaeological site in Anyang, I had the opportunity to see the teeth of an elderly woman up close. Looking at this, I found a problem.”
Researcher Liu Chi told the reporter that before studying history, he had worked in the hospital's dental department. “Experience tells me that the owner of this tooth in front of me should be more than 50 years old.”
Liu Chi said that as far as teeth are concerned, ancient people are not the same as modern people. At the same age, ancient people wore their teeth worse than modern people, because ancient foods were rougher than modern ones; they were also modern people, because of different classes and differences in food, so even at the same age, the degree of tooth wear was not the same. The older woman in Tomb No. 2 was an aristocrat in the social class at the time, so her food was relatively fine; in other words, the owner of this pair of teeth should have been older. “My judgment is that they should be at least 60 years old; the exact age figure is inconclusive.” According to this, Liu Chi has this view, “I think this woman is Mrs. Bian. Another young woman is probably her handmaid. Of course, this is on the premise that we trust the information given by the local archeology department.”
Why is there an error of almost 10 years in judging the age of the deceased from teeth? After interviewing researcher Liu Chi, with this question, the reporter also found Liu Qingzhu, a member of the Faculty of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences and former director of the Institute of Archaeology. Director Liu told the reporter that it is entirely possible for this kind of error to occur.
“The older woman in Tomb No. 2 was judged from a physical anthropological point of view, mainly based on the degree of healing of the gaps in her skull, as well as the degree of growth and wear of her teeth. Generally speaking, for children, age is relatively accurate based on bone gaps; for adults, it is less accurate and cannot be accurate to a single digit. Moreover, since physical anthropological standards for people of the yellow race have not yet been established, our current judgment criteria are based on Western, or white, standards. As a result, errors are more likely to occur.”
Liu Qingzhu also expressed doubts about Mrs. Bian's age as described in the history book: “Mrs. Bian was a diva. Before she married Cao Cao, she did not have a high social status. Ancient historians were able to report the exact year she was born. The veracity is questionable. Based on a combination of factors, I speculate that Mrs. Bian should have been in her 60's when she died, not 70 as recorded in history. So, judging from current evidence, the older woman in Tomb No. 2 is probably Mrs. Bian. Of course, after the excavation and clean-up of Objective 1, the situation may change again.”
