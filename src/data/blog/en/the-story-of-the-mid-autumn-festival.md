 The origin of the Mid-Autumn Festival
August 15 of the lunar calendar is China's traditional Mid-Autumn Festival, and it is also the second largest traditional festival in China after the Spring Festival. August 15 is right in the middle of autumn, so it is called the Mid-Autumn Festival. China's ancient calendar places August in the middle of autumn and is called “Mid-Autumn Festival,” so the Mid-Autumn Festival is also called “Mid-Autumn Festival.”
On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival, the moon was bright. The ancients regarded the full moon as a symbol of reunion, so August 15 is also known as the “Reunion Festival.” Throughout the ages, people have often used “full moon” and “lack of moon” to describe “happy and sad”. Travellers who have stayed in other countries have also used the moon to support their affectionate feelings. Poems like Tang Dynasty poet Li Bai's “Lift Your Head to the Moon, Look Down and Think Hometown”, Du Fu's “Lu Is White Tonight, the Moon Is My Hometown Ming”, and Wang Anshi of the Song Dynasty's “The Spring Breeze and the South Bank of the River, When Will the Moon Shine Again?” are all great songs from time immemorial.
Mid-Autumn Festival is an ancient festival, and enjoying the moon is an important holiday custom. Ancient emperors had a social system of spring festivals and autumn moon festivals, and private homes also had the style of celebrating the Mid-Autumn Festival moon. Later, watching the moon was more important than celebrating the moon, and serious sacrifices became a relaxed form of entertainment. The custom of watching the Moon during the Mid-Autumn Festival was very popular in the Tang Dynasty. Many famous poems by poets included Yongyue verses. The Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties court and folk moon viewing activities were even larger. Many monuments of “Moon Worship Altar,” “Moon Prayer Pavilion,” and “Mochigetsu Tower” still remain in various parts of our country. Beijing's “Moon Temple” was built during the Ming and Jiajing years for the royal lunar festival. Every time the Mid-Autumn Festival moon rises, a plan is set up in the open air to serve mooncakes, pomegranates, dates, and other melons and fruits on the table. After praying for the moon, the whole family sits around the table, talks while eating, and enjoying the bright moon together. Now, moon worship activities have been replaced by large-scale, colorful mass moonwatching amusement activities.
Eating mooncakes is another holiday custom. Mooncakes symbolize reunion. Mooncake production has been increasingly sophisticated since the Tang Dynasty. Su Dongpo wrote a poem, “A small cake is like chewing the moon, with crispy and sugar,” and Yang Guangfu in the Qing dynasty wrote, “Mooncakes are filled with peach meat filling, and ice cream is sweetened with cane sugar.” It seems that mooncakes back then are quite similar to what they are now.
According to historical records, the term “Mid-Autumn Festival” first appeared in the book “Zhou Li”. When I arrived at Wei Jin, there is a record of “confusing Shang Shu Zhen Cow; Mid-Autumn Festival Eve and the left and right fansu the river.” It wasn't until the early years of the Tang dynasty that the Mid-Autumn Festival became a fixed holiday. “Tang Shu: Taizong Ji” records the “August 15 Mid-Autumn Festival”. The popularity of the Mid-Autumn Festival began in the Song dynasty. By the Ming and Qing Dynasties, it had the same name as New Year's Day, making it one of the major festivals in our country. It is also the second-largest traditional festival in our country after the Spring Festival.
“West Lake Tour Guide” says, “August 15 is called Mid-Autumn Festival. People send mooncakes as a sign of reunion.” “An Overview of Imperial Beijing Scenery” also says, “On August 15, the cake must be round, the portion must be wrong, and the petals are engraved like a lotus flower. ... Those who have returned home to their wives will return to their husbands in Japan, called Reunion Day. On the evening of the Mid-Autumn Festival, most regions of our country also have the custom of “reunion,” that is, to bake a small cake that symbolizes reunion, similar to a mooncake. The cake is filled with sugar, sesame, osmanthus, and vegetables, and the outside is decorated with motifs such as the moon, laurel tree, and rabbit. After the lunar festival, the elderly in the family cut the cake into pieces according to the number of people, one piece for each person. If someone is not at home, they leave one for them, indicating a family reunion.
During the Mid-Autumn Festival, there are few clouds and fog, and the moonlight is clear and bright. In addition to holding a series of events such as watching the moon, celebrating the moon, and eating mooncakes to bless the reunion, some places also have activities such as dragon dancing and building pagodas. In addition to mooncakes, a variety of seasonal fresh fruits and dried fruits are also a dish for Mid-Autumn Festival night.
Another explanation for the origin of the Mid-Autumn Festival is that August 15 of the lunar calendar happens to be the time when rice is ripe, and everyone worships the Earth god. The Mid-Autumn Festival is probably the legacy of the Autumn Festival.
