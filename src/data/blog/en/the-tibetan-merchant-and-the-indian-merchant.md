 Once upon a time, there were two merchants. One is a Tibetan merchant and the other is an Indian businessman. They are all smart and do everything they can to fill up their wallets.
At one point, Indian merchants filled cans with water, labeled “canned ghee,” and shipped them to Tibet for sale. A Tibetan merchant made a batch of fake knives. The handle and scabbard were very beautiful. The knives were cut from wood and brought to India for sale. The two businessmen met halfway through. The Indian businessman saw that the Tibetan merchant's Tibetan knife was very good, and proposed to exchange it for his own ghee. They didn't expect the Tibetan merchant to fully accept it. The two soon got into this business, and they also said goodbye warmly using Indian and Tibetan manners.
The Indian businessman felt that he had taken advantage and laughed and sang as if he were drunk on the way home. He bought a coconut from the market and wanted to test the sharpness of a Tibetan sword; only then did he discover that the knife was made of wood, and he was fooled. He swore to heaven, “The Buddha testifies, I must teach this Tibetan a good lesson.” Similarly, Tibetan merchants are walking on the road. Wanting to taste the taste of fresh ghee, he ended up drinking a mouth full of dirty water, so he blushed and decided to take revenge on him.
Shortly after, they met again in their old place. Because they were selling fakes, they waved to each other even if things worked out. The Indian businessman said, “Dude, it looks like you're a smart person. I have a way to make some money, can you do it?” The Tibetan merchant said, “As long as the method is clever, of course it can be done.” The Indian merchant then told him in detail: “Dude! Listen carefully: In my hometown, there is a wealthy family. My dad is Tibetan. It's been over a year since he passed away. You pretended to be the younger brother of the deceased and promised to share some money with him while he was alive. If they agree, then they have something to say; if they don't, you cry and make a lot of noise and have to go to the grave to confirm with your brother. When the time comes, I'll dig a hole, hide in the grave, and talk to you with the voice of the dead. Look, what do you think?” The Tibetan businessman clapped their hands and praised them. The two came to India at the same time and did what was agreed upon.
The Tibetan businessman came to the rich owner's house and mentioned the name of the deceased. The hostess said, “He is my husband. It's been over a year since he passed away.” As soon as the Tibetan businessman heard it, he burst into tears: “Brother, brother! You died too soon!” The hostess was sad and surprised because she had never heard her husband mention that Tibet has a younger brother like this. The Tibetan merchant also said, “My brother promised to leave me some of my money when he was alive. I don't know where to put it?” The hostess said, “Don't talk nonsense. My husband doesn't have a younger brother like you at all, and he never promised to leave money to anyone.” The Tibetan merchant was very angry and asked, “Where is my brother buried? I want to talk to him!” The hostess said, “Everyone is dead, where else can we talk?” The Tibetan merchant said, “You don't need to worry about this; this is our Tibetan custom.” The hostess said, “Well, as long as the person in the coffin can speak, I will definitely do what he wants.”
A Tibetan merchant accompanied by her hostess came to her husband's tomb. The Tibetan merchant asked, and the Indian merchant hiding in the cave answered. The two answered questions one by one, making the hostess believe it was true. Finally, the Tibetan merchant removed a large stone, blocked the hole, followed the hostess back to the palace, and left with a large bag of gold, silver, jewels, and satin.
It took a lot of effort for the Indian merchants to push the stone and climb out of the cave, knowing that the Tibetan merchants had already left. He thought, “This Tibetan is as strong as a yak. I'm as thin as a monkey; I can't beat him; I can only use my brain to fool him.” I bought a pair of very beautiful leather boots from the market and rushed from the street to the front of the Tibetan merchant day and night. I first dropped one boot on the road, walked more than a hundred steps, then threw down another boot and hid myself in a burdock on the side of the road. Sure enough, after waiting for a while, the Tibetan merchant came over with his luggage. He saw the first boot and muttered, “Nice boot, but unfortunately there was only one!” After speaking, I continued walking forward. Seeing the second pair of boots, he shouted, “Oops, why don't I want these nice boots?” Then he put down his luggage and went back to pick up the first boot. At this point, an Indian merchant sneaked out of the burdock and ran away carrying a bag full of gold, silver, and satin.
The Tibetan merchant retrieved his boots and lost his luggage. He knew it must have been an Indian businessman doing the trick; he followed suit. Chase, chase, chase after half a day, the Indian merchant suddenly disappeared; only footprints were left on the edge of the haystack. This cunning fox must have gone into the haystack, he thought. The haystack is so big, how can I find him? It's better to come up with an idea and let him come up with his own!
At night, it was as dark as hell. A Tibetan merchant brought a pair of bells and walked around the haystack while shaking them. The Indian businessman hid in the haystack and was very happy to hear the horse bell. He thought in his heart that he would just wait for this horse to walk to his side and run out to ride. The Tibetan merchant just has four legs, so don't want to catch up with me.
Thinking about this, the horse bell just rang around him. The Indian businessman quickly ran out of the haystack and searched in the dark to catch the horse. Who knows he was caught by a big hand. If you take a close look, it's still that Tibetan merchant! He had no choice but to lower his head and confess that he was unlucky.
