 Tibetan literature and art have a long, rich and diverse history. Moreover, whether it's folk tales, folk songs, dance, or Tibetan opera, they are all unique and dazzling, and are a wonder in the motherland's 100 Gardens. Tibetan folklore is vast, and it is an important part of the rich culture of the Tibetan people.
Tibetan folk stories are divided into two categories. One is an oral story, and the other is a story compiled from written records. Among them, there are written stories such as “The Tale of Corpses,” and some people have named them “The Inexhaustible Story.” These stories have been circulating in the public for a long time, reflecting people's dissatisfaction and resistance to evil rule, praise for friendship and mutual assistance, and sympathy for free love marriage. Because of the positive significance of the stories, posterity collected their records and made them into written stories.
Among the written stories, there are also stories included in the category of aphorisms. For example, “An Explanation of Sakya's Proverbs” is a collection of 51 stories written by Ren Chinbaek and later reviewed, added, and finalized by Marton Bandhan Kuji. Also, “Gedang Sayings” collected 71 stories. There are also many stories recorded in popular Tibetan historical works, such as “The Chronicles of the King of Tibet”, “The Wedding Feast of the Wise Men”, “The History of Youth”, and “Red History,” which have recorded quite a few stories that have been handed down orally. During the recording process, the writers made many changes and artistic processing to make the story more appealing and moving.
Tibetan folk tales are very rich in content because they come from the folk world. These stories all reflect the reality of Tibetan society at the time from one side, pour out the deep love and hate of the Tibetan people, and express their strong desire for a better life. Precisely because these stories are the voices of the masses and give people courage and strength, people love to listen to them, love to spread them, and make them full of vitality.
Tibetan folk tales can generally be grouped into the following major genres:
The first is a story that praises witty people who resist oppression and exploitation. In Tibet, almost everyone knows Agudomba and Nicosambu, two smart people. They are the embodiment of ideals in the minds of the people. In the face of Agutomba, there are no insurmountable difficulties, no things they cannot do, and there are no princes, nobles, and lords who cannot be defeated. Agutomba and Nicosambu were poor serfs. They worked for the serfs and struggled in an environment of hunger and poverty. The environment was very difficult. But they did not give in or beg; instead, they used wit, bravery, intelligence, and optimism to overcome difficulties and punish vicious and greedy lords with their ingenious hands. For example, in “The Three Wise Workers,” Agutomba and Ling Li discuss the conditions for being an employee, saying that he won't shave his head for the mountains, two won't carry the sea, and three can't finish the work he has accumulated over a year in one day; all three others are fine. If the leader dismisses himself halfway, he will have to pay a year's salary. The stupid lord agreed to his terms. As a result, they told him to go up the mountain to cut firewood; if Agutonba didn't dry the jujube, it was “shaving the mountain's head”; when he went down the river, he didn't dry the jujube, it was “carrying the sea”; he said it was “a day of work he had accumulated over a year.” This made the lord lose his temper. Seeing that Agutomba couldn't do anything, they wanted to fire him. However, if he was fired halfway, he had to pay one year's salary. There was no choice but the lord had to let him take one year's salary for nothing.
It's also like the story of Nicosambu's “Combating Bad Ghee”. According to the story, the people of Shannan handed over all the ghee to the king of Shannan, so they didn't even have ghee. One day, when the wise Nikasanbu saw King Shannan walking on the balcony, he shouted a little donkey and said, “You damn thing, soak up all the milk and what shall we hand over to King Shannan?” When King Shannan heard it, he scolded Nicosambu: “Don't you know if you eat donkey ghee, people will turn into idiots?” Nicosambu said, “There really is no way. There is no beef or sheep ghee. We had to hand over donkey ghee. We had to hand over this kind of ghee before.” As a result, King Shannan ordered the “donkey” ghee to be thrown out, and Nicosambu took the opportunity to bring back all the fresh milk ghee. Listening to this kind of story made people feel awe at the clever Nicosambu, harshly hate King Shannan's rampant and overbearing and extortion claims, and rejoice and encourage his victory against poor ghee.
The witty figures Agutomba and Nicosambu are concentrated representatives of the intelligent and brave Tibetan people. Their stories inspire and inspire people, and certainly have vigorous vitality.
The second type is myths and legends and stories of war against nature. This type of story uses exaggerated methods to infuse fantasy and yearning into the story, such as the story about Tang Dongjeb, which depicts him building bridges and fighting against nature to enable people along the river to build 13 iron bridges, which facilitated transportation and kept the lives and property of the vast majority of monks and customs safe. In the story of Princess Wencheng and Princess Jincheng, people gave these two historical figures the color of myth. For example, Princess Wencheng brought in five colors of sheep: green, blue, yellow, white, and black. It also describes Princess Jincheng's determination to enter Tibet, saying that a magical mirror she was carrying was broken in a place near Xining and turned into Sun Moon Mountain. The shade blocked her way back, so she courageously moved forward and finally arrived in Lhasa. Another example is the story “The Origin of Barley Seeds”, which shaped a prince who, with the help of a mountain god, stole precious barley seeds from the Snake King, but unfortunately he was turned into a dog by the Snake King's magic. The dog brought barley seeds to the family and was loved by this girl and recovered. They worked hard, and only then did people have barley. Stories like these use the great power of myths and legends to give people wings of thought and inspire people to fight against snowy mountains, grasslands, deserts, cold winds, and bitter rain. The third category is a story reflecting the freedom of love and marriage. Stories like this reflect the voices of young people at the time. For example, “The Story of Tea and Salt,” depicts the children of two enemies of toast falling in love and vowing never to be separated. However, their parents made every effort to break up the couple. In the end, young men became salt, and young women became tea. When people drank ghee tea, salt blended with tea, and the evil lady toast could no longer be stopped. Another example is “The Blacksmith and Miss,” which reflects marriages that break through hierarchical boundaries, “The Orange Girl,” “The Frog Rider,” etc., which reflect how young men and women can fight for the right to marriage autonomy. This type of story embodies the fighting spirit of young people to achieve beautiful ideals, not being afraid of heaven or earth, not believing in gods or ghosts, defeating demons and evil, and finally getting their wishes fulfilled. Of course, there are also struggles where situations are very unfortunate. This kind of love and marriage story makes people hate an unreasonable society even more.
The fourth category, animal fables. In such stories, animals, like humans, have all kinds of virtues: bullies and weaknesses, proud and arrogant, gentle and timid, resourceful, and cunning. Among them, some help and love each other and unite against enemies; others are greedy and have no trust; they are poor neighbors. Due to the different qualities of these animals, they form a variety of contradictions and complex relationships, which have given rise to many interesting stories. For example, “The Tale of the Partridge Bird” describes the weak and kind partridge owl. From the lessons of blood, he learned the nature of foxes; he used his ingenuity, avenged, and hated them. In Tibetan animated stories, people always portray big beasts such as tigers and wolves as cruel, bossy, and foolish, while depicting the personalities of weak animals such as rabbits and lambs as being kind, witty, and brave. Through anthropomorphic descriptions of animals, these stories are easier to understand and give people a sense of philosophy, draw wisdom and strength from them, and inspire people to fight for justice.
Tibetan folk tales have their own characteristics in artistic expression. According to content needs, the genres are diverse and flexible, including myths, legends, fables, etc. From the point of view of artistic techniques, there are both obscure, straightforward, and figurative suggestions. Precisely because it uses a variety of methods, there are many but not too many.
Tibetan folk tales usually include folk songs, folk songs, and raps and songs in the narration. They are more vivid and engaging, and the stories are more sincere and rich. This is about one of the main differences between Tibetan folk tale and other folk tales.
In terms of language use, Tibetan folk tales are also common characteristics of folk tales from fraternal nations. That is, the language is simple, simple, and straightforward in narration. At the same time, many folk proverbs are used, which not only have a strong atmosphere of life, but also give people a fresh and strong sense of beauty.
