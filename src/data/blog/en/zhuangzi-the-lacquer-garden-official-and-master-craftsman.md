 Zhuangzi was once a “Mengqi Gardener,” and everyone is no stranger to this. Yang Kuan pointed out in “The History of the Warring States”: “This paint garden in Mongolia (present-day northeast of Shangqiu City, Henan Province) was managed by the Song government. The so-called 'Qi Yuan Officers' immediately managed the Qi Yuan government officials.” Is this true, is the Urushi Yuanji just a lacquer garden manager?
Among many historical documents, there is one piece of material that is very helpful for us to understand what Zhuang Zi did when he was a Qi Yuan clerk. It is a statute in the Qin Tomb Bamboo Scrolls “Qin Law Miscellany” category, which was unearthed in 1975 in Yunmeng, Hubei: “The Qi Yuan Dian has one scavenger, one shield for each of the king, the king, and zuo, and 20 for each group. When Qi Yuan was three years old, he got the second best and got the law (abolished), and ordered and emperor to rank first.” The idea is that if Qi Yuan is rated as inferior, the Chief of the Paint Shop is penalized for paying one coat of armor or money equivalent to one coat of armor, the subordinate officers (Order, General, and Zou) each pay a shield or equivalent, and the rest of the workers each pay 20 armour belts or the equivalent. If the paint garden's assessment is poor for three consecutive years, the chief in charge of the paint garden is penalized for paying two coats of armor or the same amount of money, and dismisses them, and the subordinate officials (order, castle) each pay one coat of armor or the same amount of money. As can be seen from this, there are many senior workers in the lacquer garden (stewards, lords, generals, and co-workers). The number of workers is quite large and the division of labor is clear. Therefore, a lacquer garden official is not only a manager, but also a practitioner of the entire process from lacquer tree planting to raw lacquer harvesting to lacquerware production.

Also, there is another passage that may be helpful in understanding what Zhuang Zi did when he was a “lacquer gardener”: “When Zhuangzi walked in the mountains, he saw big trees, and the foliage flourished. The lumberjacks stood by and didn't take it.” (“Yamaki”) The reason Zhuangzi went to the mountains was probably to find wood for lacquerware carving, and in “Yamaki,” he also mentioned that “a hundred year old tree” was “broken into a sacrificial bottle,” and most of the old trees are also in the mountains; furthermore, there are also “lumberjacks” in the mountains, which does not clearly explain whether they are peers or chance encounters, but at least it also indicates that valuable wood can be found in that mountain forest. The story of “Ziqing cuts wood for wood” in “Zhuangzi” also confirms this from the side (see “Zhuangzi Dasheng”). Before making lacquerware, it is necessary to go to the mountains to find suitable wood, and in order to ensure the quality of lacquerware, so that it does not warp or break, and is durable, it is necessary to carefully select it.
Through mutual verification of the above materials, we discovered that Zhuangzi, a “lacquer gardener,” knows how to make lacquerware. It is likely that he is a skilled craftsman who specializes in making lacquerware for Wang Gong's aristocrats, not just a small clerk responsible for managing the lacquer forest.
